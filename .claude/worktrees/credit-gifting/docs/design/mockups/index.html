3.2 Statistical Analyses

#1 · Causal adjustmentAs shown in Figure 2, directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) encoded a priori knowledge and assumptions, and identified scientifically meaningful and minimally sufficient covariates for estimating total effects. DAGs were created using a Structural Causal Model framework, as proposed by Pearl (2009), with Daggity.net (Textor et al., 2017). Multivariate logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs). Outcomes of interest were each of the HPV vaccine hesitancy scales and receipt, any doses, of the HPV vaccine.

Differences in low and high hesitancy were examined by gender, race and ethnicity, rurality, and the combination of rurality and Hispanic ethnicity.

5.3 Cultural Dynamics

#4 · Unsupported claimAdditionally, the levels of vaccine hesitancy seen in rural Hispanic YAs were more similar to the levels of vaccine hesitancy in White/Caucasian YAs than they were to those of urban Hispanic YAs. This suggests that rural Hispanic YAs may have knowledge and opinions on HPV vaccination that are more similar to those of their geographical neighbors of all races/ethnicities, rather than their racial/ethnic counterparts in urban areas.

5.4 Interpretation, Limitations, and Generalizability

This study had several limitations affecting the generalizability of the findings. One limitation of our study was that data regarding HPV vaccine receipt was self-reported, leaving results subject to recall bias. Participation in the survey was limited to adults with internet access, which may have excluded parts of the young adult population.

#6 · States contradictionSurvey participants were from 10 US states, but residents of Utah made up a greater proportion of the sample than other states, with 38% of responders being from Utah. Results may not be equally reflective of opinions of YAs across all Western states surveyed given that the distribution of survey participants was not equal across the 10 Western states surveyed.

Construction of the hesitancy scales
The three factor-derived scales described in Section 3.1 serve as the foundation for the substantive conclusions, yet providing readers with more evidence to trust their psychometric and conceptual security is necessary. The item descriptions indicate that the two complacency dimensions capture HPV knowledge, awareness, and benefit beliefs, which diverges from complacency in the SAGE sense of low perceived need or risk. The confidence dimension mixes confidence with convenience and access, notably including the campus-offering item.
Detailed Feedback
#1 Causal adjustment strategy is underspecified in Section 3.2

As shown in Figure 2, directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) encoded a priori knowledge and assumptions, and identified scientifically meaningful and minimally sufficient covariates for estimating total effects.

The causal framing in Section 3.2 needs more support: the text refers to total effects identified by DAGs, but does not define the exposure–outcome estimands or state the exposure-specific adjustment sets, leaving the strategy only partially reconstructable from table footnotes.
#4 Unsupported claim on relative hesitancy levels in Section 5.3

The levels of vaccine hesitancy seen in rural Hispanic YAs were more similar to the levels in White/Caucasian YAs than to those of urban Hispanic YAs.

Using the displayed high-hesitancy proportions, rural Hispanic YAs appear closer to urban Hispanic YAs for some dimensions; the intended comparator or similarity metric should be clarified.
#6 Contradiction in the number of Western states surveyed

Survey participants were from 10 US states, but residents of Utah made up a greater proportion of the sample, with 38% of responders being from Utah.

The geographic scope is internally inconsistent: the abstract and Section 2.1 describe participants across 12 Western states, but Section 5.4 says 10 US states. The final analytic sample should be reported consistently.
